# Page structures

Use these outlines as a starting point and drop sections that would be empty. Each page opens with one or two sentences that tell the reader what the page is for.

## How-to guide

Title: "How to <do the task>".

1. Purpose: what the reader can do after following the guide, and when to use it.
2. Before you start: access, tools, versions and inputs required.
3. Steps: numbered, one action per step, exact commands or settings in code blocks, expected result after key steps.
4. Check it worked: how to confirm success.
5. If something goes wrong: symptoms and fixes, and who to ask.
6. Related pages.

## Decision record

Title: "Decision: <what was decided>".

1. Status and date: proposed, accepted, superseded or rejected; the date; who decided.
2. Context: the problem and the forces that made a decision necessary.
3. Options considered: each option with its benefits and costs.
4. Decision: the choice in one or two sentences.
5. Rationale: why this option beat the others, with evidence.
6. Consequences: what gets better, what gets worse, what new work it creates.
7. Follow-through: tasks, owners and how the team will know whether it worked.
8. Supersedes or superseded by: links both ways.

## FAQ entry

Title: the question as people actually ask it.

1. Short answer: one to three sentences.
2. Details: the explanation, conditions and exceptions.
3. Example.
4. Related questions.

## Explainer

Title: the concept's name, or "What is <concept>".

1. In one paragraph: definition and why it matters here.
2. How it works.
3. Examples from this organization's work.
4. Common misunderstandings.
5. Related pages.

## Reference page

Title: the subject and "reference", for example "Billing settings reference".

1. Scope: what the table or list covers and its source of truth.
2. The reference content as tables or definition lists.
3. Change history.

## Lesson learned or post-incident review

Title: "<Event> review" with the date.

1. Summary: what happened and the impact, without blame.
2. Timeline.
3. What went well and what did not.
4. Contributing causes.
5. Actions: each with an owner and a due date.
6. What we would do differently.

## Metadata

Set as database properties when the page lives in a database, otherwise as a short line under the title: owner, status, last reviewed, tags, source (conversation date, meeting or thread).

## Create or update

- Update the existing page when it covers the same subject for the same audience, or when the new material corrects or extends it.
- Create a new page when the content will be linked or searched for on its own, or serves a different audience.
- For significant updates, add a dated line to a "Change history" section: what changed and why.
- Superseded decisions keep their page; change the status and link the new decision.

## Writing for discovery

- Use the words readers will search for in the title and the first paragraph.
- Keep paragraphs short; prefer lists and tables for scannable content.
- Describe images in text so search and screen readers can use them.
- Link to the hub page and to two or three related pages.
